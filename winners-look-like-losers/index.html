Research note · Nasdaq intraday momentum · November 2026

Winners and losers look identical at entry

Five tests on about 8,500 backtest trades and 380 live (demo) trades, and what they say about where a momentum edge really lives.

Vivaan Desai · 17, still in school · I built and run this system myself, on a demo account, and publish the results that didn't work too. PDF version

Results in brief

  • 1Nothing visible at the moment of entry separates future winners from losers. I checked 23 features of the market and of the entry bar across two backtest periods and live trading. One held up in all three, and the system already used it.
  • 2The outcome shows up quickly once the trade is on, but only through price. Fifteen minutes in, profit or loss alone separates final winners from losers with an AUC of about 0.75–0.79. The shape of the path adds at most 0.03.
  • 3Calendar flows that are supposed to be predictable have faded. A month-end rebalancing effect that was clear in 2012–18 reversed in 2019–26.
  • 4Machine learning found nothing the simple model missed. Gradient-boosted trees with 1-minute features scored an out-of-sample AUC of 0.528, and 0.497 on live trades.
  • 5An unsupervised regime model learned quiet versus busy markets. It did not learn trend versus chop, and it told me nothing about the next four hours.
  • →The edge is in the shape of the payoff: cut losers fast, keep the trades that move +1R in your favour. About 3 in 10 trades get there, and they earn more than the whole system nets.

The system, without the rules

It trades the Nasdaq 100 index CFD on 15-minute bars in both directions, entering in the direction of a move that is already under way. Every trade has a stop scaled to recent volatility (ATR), so a loss of one stop is called −1R. Losers that don't work quickly are cut, and winners are trailed. Every trade risks the same fraction of the account, with five simple size rules on top.

It has run in real time on a demo account since July 2026. Each week I replay the same week in the backtest engine and match trades one by one. In the weeks checked so far, 87–100% of trades matched, so the backtest numbers below describe the same system that trades live.

I don't publish the entry or exit rules. Everything here is about what the trades show, not how to copy them.

Test 1

What the market looked like at the fill

nothing consistent

I grouped trades by 11 market traits at the moment of the fill: time of day, volatility, how far the day had moved, trend over 20 days, position in the 12-hour range and more. I added 12 features of the entry bar itself, such as body, wick and where it closed. For each, I compared the top third of trades with the bottom third, in live trades and in two backtest periods (2017–19 and 2022–26).

One feature pointed the same way in all three samples, and the system already sized trades on it. The rest flipped sign between periods or were inside the noise. Before the fill, winners and losers had travelled the same path: both entered after a run of about 2 ATR in their direction.

Test 2

How fast does the market reveal the outcome?

only through price

At 15, 30, 60 and 120 minutes after entry I asked how well the trade's current profit or loss predicts whether it ends as a winner (AUC: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is certain). Then I asked whether the shape of the path adds anything: bar sizes, how clean the move was, how many bars went against the trade, other indices.

The steep curve looks like the market revealing a hidden answer. It isn't. From the one-hour mark, holding for four more hours is worth about zero in every profit-or-loss bucket (between −0.07R and +0.08R), with one exception: trades already above +1R keep going (+0.12R and +0.17R in the two periods). After the fill the outcome is built bar by bar, and the curve mostly measures R already banked. One surprise went against my own idea: given the same profit, bigger bars after entry meant a slightly worse hold, not a better one.

Test 3

Who has to trade after me?

the footprints faded

If nothing on the chart predicts the outcome, maybe the calendar does. Some traders must buy or sell on a timetable: pension funds rebalancing at month end, money arriving at the turn of the month, options dealers hedging into monthly expiry, volatility-target funds cutting exposure after volatility jumps. I first checked whether each flow leaves a footprint in the index itself, using daily data from 2012 to 2026.

The month-end effect was real until 2018, then reversed. The monthly options pin was visible intraday in 2017–19: on expiry Wednesday to Friday the day was less trendy (t −2.18). It disappeared in 2022–26, after options expiring every day took over. Turn-of-month and volatility-target flows left no footprint in Nasdaq. Flows with a public timetable get traded ahead of, and the ones that still pay are hard to time.

Test 4

Can machine learning pick the winners?

no

This is meta-labeling: given that the system has fired, predict whether the trade becomes a runner (ends at +1R or better). I used gradient-boosted trees with fixed settings and no tuning, 8 features from the 15-minute bars plus 11 from 1-minute bars (path efficiency, direction flips, tick volume against its usual level for that minute, signed volume, distance to VWAP and to the opening range). Validation used an expanding walk-forward with a 48-hour purge, training on one period and testing on the other, and the live trades.

There is a faint real signal: the model beats shuffled labels (p = 0.015), and the top decile has more runners than the bottom one. But the 1-minute data added nothing (−0.005). The model's probabilities were worse than simply predicting the average. Sizing trades up or down by its score lowered risk-adjusted returns in 2022–26 and on live trades. A check that leaks 5 minutes of the future into one feature scored 0.64, while the same feature from 5 minutes before the fill scored 0.51, so the setup could see real information if it were there.

Test 5

Does the market have hidden moods?

activity, not direction

Last, I let a 3-state hidden Markov model learn market "moods" from six measurements of each 15-minute bar, without ever seeing a trade. It was refitted every month on the past year and read forward only, in both periods.

It learned what persists, which is activity. The state it was supposed to find, trend versus chop, doesn't persist and isn't hidden in these measurements. Next-four-hour efficiency is the same in every state (backtest 2022–26; 2017–19 is the same within 0.01).

So where is the edge?

Direction at entry is close to a coin flip. The edge is the asymmetry: most trades are cut small, and the 3 in 10 that reach +1R nearly all end as winners and carry the whole result. The best 5% of trades earn more than four times the system's net. So the useful questions are about holding and sizing those trades, about costs, and about adding independent strategies, not about predicting which trade will win.

How I tested

  • Every test was written down before it ran: the question, the decision it feeds, and the pass or fail rule.
  • Two backtest periods, 2017–19 and 2022–26, plus live (demo) trades. A result counts only if it holds in both periods and points the same way live.
  • Positive controls: plant a fake signal of known size and check the test finds it. A null only counts if the test could have seen something.
  • Shuffled-label nulls and a future-leak check for the machine-learning work.
  • Every variant tried is counted, and verdicts are works, fails or undetermined, with the smallest effect each test could detect.

Limits

  • The account is a demo with real prices. Live results so far show about half the backtest's edge per trade, over a few months and several system versions.
  • One market, one broker's CFD, intraday only. Costs matter: the spread is roughly as large as the edge per trade.
  • All numbers marked backtest come from my own engine. It matches live trades closely in the weeks checked, but it is still a model.
